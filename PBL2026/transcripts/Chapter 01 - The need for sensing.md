**Task 11: What Do You Think?**

Imagine, for a moment, a person who has no functioning sense organs. What could you say about the life of such a person?

One thing is for sure: **information is fundamental to how we understand and interact with the world.** The more information we can obtain and the better we are able to connect, interpret, and learn from that information, the better we can understand and respond to our environment. This is one of the reasons AI systems are trained using large amounts of data.

Take a human being, for instance. Our sense organs help us interact with and understand our environment. They detect different stimuli from the environment, and the resulting signals are transmitted through the nervous system to different parts of the brain for processing. Some of this information also contributes, directly or indirectly, to how we respond to situations, including our fight-or-flight response.

And one thing you can be fairly certain of is that a person without functioning senses would have great difficulty interacting with the environment, learning from it, detecting danger, and surviving independently.

Humans have five commonly recognised senses: **sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch**, associated primarily with the eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and skin. However, these organs are not uni-purpose. They perform multiple functions, although each has functions that are more fundamental to its role.

Take the eyes, for instance. With your eyes:

1. You can see the colour of an object.
2. You can detect light and darkness.
3. You can detect motion.
4. You can detect potential danger.
5. With a trained eye, you can even read lips and obtain information about speech visually.
6. You can estimate the speed and direction of moving objects.

However, the fundamental role of the eye is to **detect light and provide the visual system with information carried by that light**. Many of the other things we are able to do with vision result from how the visual system processes and interprets this information.

The human eye, for instance, receives light from its environment as a stimulus. But this is not just ordinary light. The light carries information about the environment.

The **cornea and lens work together to focus incoming light onto the retina**. The retina contains specialised light-sensitive cells called **photoreceptors**, which respond to the light and initiate neural signals. These signals are then transmitted through the optic nerve and other parts of the visual pathway to areas of the brain involved in vision, where the information is processed.

So, what we see is not simply the light itself. What we experience as vision is the result of the visual system receiving, processing, and interpreting information carried by light.

This means that defects in the eye can affect how the visual information is received. For example, if the optical system of the eye does not properly focus the light onto the retina, or if the retina or other parts of the eye are affected, the information generated by the visual system may be altered.

But the eye is only one part of the process.

Once the brain receives visual information, it has to process it and make sense of what we are perceiving. This processing is influenced not only by the information arriving from the eyes, but also by previous experiences, learning, memory, and information from our other senses.

Think about a child seeing sugar for the first time.

The child may see the object, but simply seeing it does not automatically mean that the child knows what it is called, how it tastes, how it smells, or how it feels.

Learning creates these associations.

Someone tells the child, “This is called sugar,” and an association begins to form between the visual appearance of the object and the name **sugar**. The child can then obtain more information through the other senses.

How does it taste?

How does it smell?

How does it feel?

Does it make a sound?

Over time, these different pieces of information become connected through learning and experience. So, the next time the child's eyes register a familiar object that looks like sugar, the brain can draw on previous experience to recognise it and anticipate some of its properties.

This is a simplified way of thinking about how humans are able to interface with and make sense of our environment.

Three things are very important because they can influence the quality and accuracy of the information that eventually reaches our perception.

Ignoring, for the moment, everything that has to do with the external environment, the **sense organ itself** can introduce problems. If the eye has a defect, for example, the quality of the visual information reaching the rest of the visual system can be affected.

The **transmission pathway** can also introduce problems. If there is an issue somewhere along the nervous pathway carrying the information from the eye toward the brain, the information reaching the relevant processing areas can be affected.

Finally, the **processing system** can also introduce problems. If there is an issue with the parts of the brain responsible for processing visual information, the way that information is interpreted can be affected.

Although these are biological processes, they are very analogous to how we obtain information from our environment in order to measure and quantify physical quantities.

A problem with the sensing element can affect the information we obtain. A problem in the path through which that information travels can affect what reaches the processing stage. And a problem in the processing stage can affect the result we eventually obtain.

The same general idea applies to electronic measurement.

The components, circuits, techniques, and methods we employ can all affect the quality of the information represented by our measurements.
