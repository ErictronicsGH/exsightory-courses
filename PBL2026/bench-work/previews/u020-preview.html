Card 2 · Three converters side by side


FigureThe owner's comparison table, drawn as our own figure, with the values checked against the three datasheets in the spec. The poe comes first; then Drive spotlights the resolution row, then the rate row, with rank chips. F3: the two rankings are not opposite. Resolution runs 16 > 12 > 10, and speed runs ESP32 > ATmega328P > ADS1115. The most bits sit on by far the slowest part, but between the two SARs the finer one is also the faster.






Rates: the ATmega328P reaches “up to 76.9 kSPS, up to 15 kSPS at maximum resolution” (its datasheet). The classic ESP32 has no single figure; its continuous (DMA) mode runs to hundreds of kSPS. The table's cell says “depends on mode”, so the rank chip says “fastest” without a number. The ESP32's differential cell (“Not the same differential-input architecture…”) is vague. Its SAR units are single-ended in Espressif's ADC documentation, and the spec sources this before the figure is drawn (N1).

Card 6 · Can the bus keep up?


EquationThe PLAN wants the payload term first (samples/s × 16 bits), then the I²C overhead switched on (27 clocks per read), against markers at 100 kHz and 400 kHz. Proposed: one log–log line plot of the required clock against samples per second, live. The two numerics land on it: 160 kbit/s, then 270 kHz.

27 clocks means the controller has already set the pointer register to the conversion register; it stays there until the next write, which a continuous-mode reading loop never makes. Then each read is an address byte and two data bytes, each with its ACK. START, STOP and the gaps between transactions come on top, as the explain says. F5: the chain this card shows is word for word D2·010 card 1's, so “extend” promises an addition the room won't find.
